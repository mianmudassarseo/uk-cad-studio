HMO conversion drawings

HMO drawings that satisfy planning, licensing and building control.

Layouts designed around room size standards, amenity ratios and fire strategy — so the scheme you fund is the scheme the licensing officer will actually sign off.

Get a free project reviewCall +92 305 6205832

HMO conversion is the one residential sector where a beautiful layout can be completely worthless. A room that measures 6.2 square metres is not a bedroom in licensing terms. Seven tenants sharing one bathroom will fail an amenity check. A protected escape route that has been designed around the furniture rather than the regulations will be picked apart on inspection.

We draw HMOs for landlords, investors and developers with the three approvals in mind from the first sketch: planning (which increasingly means an Article 4 application), the HMO licence with its room size and amenity standards, and building control with its fire strategy. Getting all three to agree is the actual job.

Room sizes and amenity standards

Mandatory HMO licensing in England applies to properties occupied by five or more people forming two or more households. Under those national conditions, sleeping rooms have statutory minimum floor areas — broadly 6.51 square metres for one person over ten, 10.22 square metres for two people over ten, and 4.64 square metres for a child under ten. Floor area under a ceiling height of 1.5 metres does not count toward the total.

Those are the national minimums. Most councils also operate their own additional or selective licensing schemes with higher standards — larger rooms, minimum kitchen worktop and storage per person, and set ratios of WCs, wash basins and bathrooms to occupants. Those local standards are what actually catch schemes out, and they differ significantly between authorities.

We design to the standards published by the specific council your property sits in, and we annotate every room with its net usable floor area on the drawing so the licensing officer can check it without a scale rule.

Planning, Article 4 and use classes

A small HMO for three to six unrelated occupants falls into use class C4, and changing from a family dwelling (C3) to C4 is permitted development in principle. The catch is that a large and growing number of councils have made Article 4 directions removing that right, often across entire wards or the whole local authority area — especially in university cities.

Large HMOs of seven or more occupants are sui generis and always need a full planning application, wherever they are.

We check the Article 4 position for your address at the outset, because it changes the entire route: an Article 4 area means a planning application with a design and access statement, and often a case to make about housing mix and concentration of HMOs in the street.

Fire strategy and building control

The fire strategy is where HMO drawings earn their keep. A converted HMO typically needs a protected escape route from every storey to a final exit, fire-rated doors with self-closers to rooms opening onto that route, fire-rated ceilings and floors between units, an appropriate grade and category of interlinked alarm system, and emergency lighting on longer escape routes.

We mark fire door ratings, compartment lines, alarm positions and escape routes directly on the plans, and produce a separate fire strategy drawing where the property has three or more storeys — which is exactly what your licensing officer and building control body will ask to see.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to the questions we are asked most often.

What is the minimum bedroom size for an HMO?

Under mandatory HMO licensing conditions in England the national minimums are 6.51 square metres for one person aged over ten, 10.22 square metres for two people aged over ten, and 4.64 square metres for a child under ten. Floor space with a ceiling height below 1.5 metres does not count. Many councils set higher standards through additional licensing, so we always design to your specific local authority's published figures.

Do I need planning permission to convert a house into an HMO?

For three to six occupants the change from C3 to C4 is permitted development nationally, but a large number of councils have removed that right using Article 4 directions, which means a full planning application. Any HMO for seven or more occupants is sui generis and always requires planning permission. We check the Article 4 position for your address before quoting.

What is the difference between an HMO licence and planning permission?

They are entirely separate. Planning permission concerns whether the change of use is acceptable in that location. An HMO licence concerns whether the property is safe and adequately equipped for the number of people living in it — room sizes, amenities, fire safety and management. You can hold one and still be refused the other, which is why we design against both from the start.

Do you produce fire strategy drawings for HMOs?

Yes. We mark escape routes, compartmentation lines, fire door ratings, alarm system grade and category, and emergency lighting positions on the drawings, and produce a standalone fire strategy sheet for properties of three storeys or more.

Can you help with a licensing application after refusal?

Often, yes. Where a scheme has been turned down on room sizes, amenity ratios or fire provision, we can survey the property, identify precisely which standard was not met, and redraw a compliant layout. Sometimes the fix is minor; sometimes it means reducing the room count, and we will tell you which it is.

How many bathrooms does an HMO need?

Most councils work to a ratio based on occupancy — commonly one bathroom and WC per set number of sharers, with a separate WC required above certain occupancy levels. The exact ratio is set by your local authority's amenity standards rather than by national law, so we confirm it against the published standards for your area before finalising the layout.
