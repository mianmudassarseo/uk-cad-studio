Land Registry lease plans: getting them right first time

A rejected lease plan does not just cost a redraw. It costs weeks, at the precise point in a transaction when nobody has weeks to spare.

Lease plans 12 May 2026 7 min read

Lease plans are the smallest drawings we produce and generate a disproportionate share of the urgency. They sit on the critical path of a leasehold sale or a new lease grant, and when HM Land Registry raises a requisition against one, the transaction simply stops until it is fixed.

Almost all of those requisitions are avoidable. The requirements are published and unambiguous — the problem is that lease plans are frequently produced by people who have not read them.

What the Land Registry requires

A plan submitted to support registration of a lease must meet a set of specific criteria.

  • Drawn to a stated metric scale. Typically 1:100 or 1:200 for an individual flat, 1:500 or 1:1250 for larger sites and location plans. The scale must be written on the drawing.
  • Accurate and to scale. A plan marked "not to scale" or "for illustrative purposes only" will not be accepted.
  • A north point. On every plan showing the demise.
  • Floor level identification. For a flat in a multi-storey building, the plan must state which storey or storeys the demise occupies.
  • A clearly defined demise. The extent of the leased property must be unambiguous, conventionally edged in red.
  • Rights and reservations shown. Parking spaces, access routes, gardens, bin and cycle stores must be identified, usually by distinct colours or hatching with a key.
  • Identifiable on the Ordnance Survey base. Which is why a 1:1250 location plan is normally supplied alongside the detailed demise plan.

The errors that cause requisitions

Using an estate agent's floor plan

By far the most common. Marketing floor plans are drawn to sell a property, not to define a legal boundary. They are frequently approximate, rarely carry a stated scale or north point, and almost never define the demise clearly. They are not lease plans and should not be submitted as such.

Scale problems

A plan drawn at 1:100 on A3 and then printed on A4 is no longer at 1:100, and the stated scale is now wrong. This happens constantly. A scale bar drawn on the sheet solves it, because the bar shrinks with the drawing.

Ambiguous demise

If it is not immediately obvious whether a balcony, a loft space, a hallway storage cupboard, a section of garden or a bin store falls inside or outside the red line, the Registry will ask. Every boundary of the demise needs to be unambiguous — including internal ones where the demise abuts communal space.

Colours that do not match the lease

If the lease text grants a right of way over land "shown edged blue" and the plan shows it hatched green, that is a requisition. The plan and the lease wording must be checked against each other before submission — a step that is skipped more often than it should be.

Missing floor identification

For flats, the storey must be stated. "First floor flat" in the lease heading is not the same as the plan identifying the first floor, and the Registry wants it on the plan.

Where a lease is being granted over part of a building, it is worth having the plan reviewed against the draft lease wording before the lease is engrossed. Correcting a plan is straightforward; correcting a plan after the lease has been signed is considerably less so.

When you need one

A compliant plan is required where a lease is registrable — broadly, leases granted for more than seven years, and certain other categories including leases with a delayed start date. It will also be needed on a lease extension, on a new-build leasehold sale, and where a lease is being varied in a way that affects the extent of the demise.

Leases of seven years or less are generally not registrable, so a registration-compliant plan may not be strictly required. A clear, accurate plan is still strongly advisable — it defines what has actually been let, and it prevents the kind of dispute that surfaces years later when someone puts a shed on a piece of land nobody had thought about.

What a good lease plan pack contains

  1. A demise plan at 1:100 or 1:200 showing the leased area edged red, with room layout, north point, stated scale and scale bar, and floor level clearly identified.
  2. A location plan at 1:1250 on an Ordnance Survey base, identifying the building within its street context.
  3. Where relevant, a site or ground floor plan showing parking spaces, gardens, bin and cycle stores and access routes, colour-keyed to the lease wording.
  4. Where the building has multiple storeys within the demise, a separate plan for each floor, each individually labelled.

How long it takes

For a straightforward flat where the property can be accessed for survey, a compliant plan can normally be produced within a few working days. Where accurate existing drawings are available and reliable, it can be faster still.

The thing that adds time is discovering on site that the property does not match the drawings you were given — extensions that were never recorded, internal walls that have moved, a demise that includes a section of loft nobody mentioned. That is precisely why the survey matters, and why the cheapest plan is rarely the one that gets registered first time.
