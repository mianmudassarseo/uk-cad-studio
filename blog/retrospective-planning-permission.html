Retrospective Planning Permission: How to Put Unauthorised Work Right

Built something without planning permission, or not quite as approved? Here is how retrospective applications work in England, what the council can do, and how to give your application the best chance.

Planning Updated 5 October 2026 13 min read
Quick answer

Retrospective planning permission is a normal planning application made after the work has already been built, allowed under section 73A of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. You submit the same drawings and pay the same fee as you would have before building. Building without permission is not a criminal offence in itself, but the council can refuse the application and serve an enforcement notice, and ignoring that notice is an offence. In England, councils now generally have 10 years to take enforcement action against unauthorised building work completed on or after 25 April 2024.

Most retrospective applications start with a letter. A neighbour complains, a buyer's solicitor asks for the planning history, or a council officer spots an extension that is not on the system. The good news is that a large share of unauthorised work can be put right with a well-prepared application. The bad news is that a weak one can turn a fixable problem into an order to take the work down.

This guide explains how retrospective planning permission works in England, what drawings you need, the enforcement risks, and what to do if the council says no.

Key takeaways

  • A retrospective application is assessed on its planning merits, exactly as if the work had not been built.
  • The fee and drawings are the same as a normal application, but the drawings must show what was actually built.
  • Building without permission is not a crime, but failing to comply with an enforcement notice is.
  • For most work completed on or after 25 April 2024, the enforcement time limit is 10 years.
  • If the time limit has passed, a lawful development certificate for an existing use may be the better route.
  • Planning and building regulations are separate. Unapproved building work may also need a regularisation certificate.

What is retrospective planning permission?

Retrospective planning permission is permission granted for development that has already been carried out without it. Section 73A of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 lets the council grant permission for work that has already happened, including work that did not follow the approved drawings or broke a condition.

Councils are told to judge these applications on their planning merits. Officers ask the same questions they would for a new proposal: is the design acceptable, what is the impact on neighbours, and does it meet local policy? The fact that the work is already built should not count for or against it, although in practice a scheme that has already caused complaints gets close scrutiny.

Enforcement: what the council can do

Building without planning permission is not a criminal offence on its own. It becomes one if the council serves an enforcement notice and you do not comply with it. Councils have several tools:

  • Planning contravention notice: a formal request for information about the work and its use.
  • Enforcement warning notice: introduced by recent reforms, inviting a retrospective application within a set period.
  • Enforcement notice: requires specific steps, such as altering or removing the work, by a deadline. You can appeal it to the Planning Inspectorate.
  • Stop notice or temporary stop notice: halts ongoing work quickly.
  • Breach of condition notice: requires compliance with a condition, with no right of appeal.

Enforcement is discretionary. Councils are expected to act proportionately, and many invite a retrospective application before taking formal action. Responding quickly and cooperatively almost always leads to a better outcome.

Time limits changed in 2024. Under the Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023, councils in England generally have 10 years to take enforcement action against unauthorised building work and changes of use completed on or after 25 April 2024. Work substantially completed before that date keeps the older 4 year limit for operational development and changes of use to a single dwelling.

How to apply for retrospective planning permission

  1. Check the facts. Confirm exactly what was built, when it was substantially completed, and what the planning history says.
  2. Talk to the council. If an enforcement officer is involved, tell them you intend to apply and agree a timescale.
  3. Commission accurate as-built drawings. They must show the work as it actually exists, measured on site, not as it was originally intended.
  4. Prepare supporting documents. A planning statement explaining why the work is acceptable, and any reports the council needs, such as a heritage statement in a conservation area.
  5. Submit through the Planning Portal with the form, fee and ownership certificate, ticking that the work has already started or been completed.
  6. Respond to comments and be ready to offer changes, such as obscure glazing or a lower roof, that make the scheme acceptable.

What drawings a retrospective application needs

The drawing set is the same as for a normal application, but accuracy matters even more, because the officer can visit and measure what is there. A typical householder retrospective set includes:

How much does retrospective planning permission cost?

The council fee is the same as for an equivalent normal application. Householder fees are set nationally and change periodically, so check the current figure on the Planning Portal. On top of that, budget for:

  • As-built drawings, which for a simple extension are usually in the same range as planning drawings, around £1,000 to £2,500 according to published 2026 guides.
  • A planning statement or consultant advice if the case is contentious.
  • Any specialist reports the council asks for.
  • The cost of alterations if the council will only approve a modified version.

What happens if retrospective planning permission is refused?

A refusal does not automatically mean demolition, but it usually leads to enforcement. Your options are:

  • Amend and resubmit. Address the reasons for refusal, for example by reducing height or changing materials.
  • Appeal the refusal to the Planning Inspectorate. Householder appeals generally need to be made within 12 weeks of the decision.
  • Appeal an enforcement notice if one is served, on the grounds set out in the Act, such as the steps required being excessive. Recent reforms limit your ability to re-argue the planning merits through an enforcement appeal once they have already been considered, so the original application needs to be strong.
  • Comply with the notice by altering or removing the work within the deadline.

If you are unsure, take advice before the appeal deadline passes. Missing it removes options.

Selling a house with unauthorised work

Buyers' solicitors check the planning history. Where work lacks permission, the usual choices are a retrospective application, a lawful development certificate if the time limit has passed, or indemnity insurance. Indemnity insurance is cheap but normally becomes void if anyone contacts the council about the work, and it does not make the work lawful. For anything recent or visible, regularising it properly is usually the safer choice.

Do not forget building regulations

Planning and building regulations are separate systems. Work built without building regulations approval can often be regularised with a regularisation certificate from the council for unauthorised work carried out on or after 11 November 1985. This normally means opening up parts of the work for inspection and providing drawings showing how it was built. Our guide to building regulations drawings explains what building control needs, and planning permission vs building regulations sets out the difference.

When a lawful development certificate is better

If the work has been in place longer than the relevant enforcement time limit without action being taken, it may have become immune from enforcement. In that case you can apply for a lawful development certificate for an existing use or development instead of planning permission. You will need evidence such as dated photographs, invoices, council tax records or statutory declarations proving when the work was completed. Deliberate concealment can extend the time limit, so the evidence needs to be honest and complete.

Frequently asked questions

What is retrospective planning permission?

It is a planning application made after the work has already been carried out, allowed under section 73A of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. The council assesses it on its planning merits as if the work had not been built.

Is it illegal to build without planning permission?

Building without planning permission is not a criminal offence in itself. It becomes an offence if the council serves an enforcement notice and you fail to comply with it by the deadline.

How much does retrospective planning permission cost?

The council fee is the same as for a normal application of the same type. You also need as-built drawings, which for a simple extension typically cost around £1,000 to £2,500, plus any reports the council asks for.

How long does the council have to take enforcement action?

In England, councils generally have 10 years to act against unauthorised building work and changes of use completed on or after 25 April 2024. Work substantially completed before that date may still benefit from the older 4 year limit.

What happens if retrospective planning permission is refused?

You can amend and resubmit, appeal the refusal to the Planning Inspectorate, or comply with any enforcement notice. A householder appeal generally needs to be made within 12 weeks of the decision.

Can I sell a house with an extension that has no planning permission?

Yes, but the buyer's solicitor will raise it. The usual solutions are a retrospective application, a lawful development certificate if the time limit has passed, or indemnity insurance, which does not make the work lawful.

Do I also need building regulations approval?

Yes, if the work needed it. Unapproved building work carried out on or after 11 November 1985 can often be put right with a regularisation certificate from the council.
