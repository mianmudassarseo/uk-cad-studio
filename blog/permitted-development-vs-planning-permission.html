Permitted development or planning permission? How to tell

Permitted development is not a loophole, and it is not a guarantee. It is a set of specific numerical limits that are easier to breach than most homeowners expect.

Planning 04 March 2026 9 min read

Permitted development rights are a national grant of planning permission, given in advance, for certain types of building work. If your project stays inside the limits, you do not need to apply to the council. If it steps outside them by so much as a hundred millimetres, you need full planning permission for the whole thing.

That binary quality is what makes permitted development worth understanding properly. It is not a spectrum. You are either inside the box or outside it.

The main extension allowances

For single-storey rear extensions on a house, the standard allowance is three metres beyond the original rear wall for an attached house — terraced or semi-detached — and four metres for a detached house. Maximum height is four metres, and where the extension comes within two metres of a boundary the eaves height is capped at three metres.

There is also a larger allowance available through a prior approval process, which roughly doubles those depths to six metres for attached and eight metres for detached houses. It is not automatic: you notify the council, they consult your adjoining neighbours, and if a neighbour objects the council decides whether the impact is acceptable. It is faster and cheaper than a full application, but it is not a certainty.

Across all of it sits the 50 per cent rule: the total area covered by buildings must not exceed half the area of land around the original house. "Original" means as it stood on 1 July 1948, or as first built if later — so previous owners' extensions count against your allowance, even ones you never saw.

Loft conversions

Loft conversions have their own volume-based allowance: broadly 40 cubic metres of additional roof space for a terraced house and 50 cubic metres for a semi-detached or detached house. Again, previous roof enlargements count against the total.

  • No extension beyond the plane of the existing roof slope on the principal elevation facing a highway — which rules out front dormers under permitted development.
  • No part of the extension may be higher than the highest part of the existing roof, so the ridge cannot be raised.
  • Materials must be similar in appearance to the existing house.
  • Side-facing windows must be obscure-glazed and, if less than 1.7 metres above the floor, non-opening.
  • Roof extensions are not permitted development at all in conservation areas, National Parks, AONBs or the Broads.

When permitted development rights do not exist

This is where most of the trouble lives. Permitted development rights are removed or restricted in a number of situations, and any one of them is enough.

  • Flats and maisonettes have no permitted development rights at all. Not reduced — none. Every alteration needs an application.
  • Listed buildings require listed building consent for most alterations, internal as well as external.
  • Conservation areas, National Parks, AONBs and the Broads have reduced rights: side extensions and roof extensions lose permitted development status, cladding is restricted, and the larger prior approval extension route is unavailable.
  • Article 4 directions allow a council to withdraw specific permitted development rights across a defined area. These are widely used for HMO conversions and, in some places, for front extensions and roof alterations.
  • Planning conditions on newer estates frequently remove permitted development rights entirely as a condition of the original consent. This is extremely common on estates built in the last thirty years and is easy to miss.
  • Previous extensions that have already used up the volume or curtilage allowance.
Check your title deeds and the original planning permission for your estate before assuming permitted development applies. A condition removing those rights will not show up on any map — it sits in the decision notice for the estate, sometimes decades old.

Permitted development does not mean unregulated

This is the misconception that causes the most expensive problems. Permitted development removes the need for planning permission. It has no effect whatsoever on:

  • Building regulations approval, which is required for virtually all extension and conversion work regardless of the planning route.
  • The Party Wall etc. Act 1996, where you are building on or near a shared boundary.
  • Restrictive covenants in your title deeds, which are a private legal matter entirely separate from planning.
  • Protected trees and hedgerows, which need separate consent to fell or prune.

Why get a Lawful Development Certificate?

A Lawful Development Certificate — an LDC — is a formal application to the council asking them to confirm in writing that your proposed works are lawful without planning permission. It is not compulsory, and plenty of people build under permitted development without one.

It is still usually worth it, for three reasons. First, it is a genuine legal safeguard: if the council later disputes whether the work was permitted, a certificate settles it. Second, buyers' conveyancing solicitors increasingly ask for evidence that works were lawful, and without a certificate the burden of proving it falls on you at the worst possible moment. Third, the application fee is normally a fraction of a full householder application.

The catch is that an LDC application requires accurate, fully dimensioned drawings — the council is being asked to certify specific measurements, so approximate drawings will not do.

How to decide which route you are on

  1. Establish the property type. If it is a flat, stop — you need planning permission.
  2. Check for designations: conservation area, listed status, National Park, AONB, Article 4 direction. Your council's online planning map will show most of these.
  3. Check the planning history of the property and the estate for conditions removing permitted development rights.
  4. Measure what has already been built. Previous extensions count against your allowance.
  5. Measure the proposal accurately against the limits — depth, height, eaves height, boundary distance, coverage.
  6. If it is inside the limits, consider an LDC. If it is outside, or if any of the above is uncertain, plan for a full application.

The honest answer is that most properties need step two and three checked by someone who does it regularly, because the information is scattered across the council's planning map, the property's planning history and the title deeds. It is a short piece of work that routinely saves months.
