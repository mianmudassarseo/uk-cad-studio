Permitted Development vs Planning Permission: How to Tell Which You Need

Permitted development is planning permission that already exists, with strict limits that are easy to breach. Here are the 2026 rules for extensions, lofts, porches and garden rooms, and the situations where you need a planning application instead.

Planning Updated 28 October 2026 Published 4 March 2026 14 min read
Quick answer

Permitted development is planning permission granted in advance by national law for certain work to houses in England, so you do not need to apply to the council if the project stays inside the limits. Typical examples are a single storey rear extension up to 3 m deep on an attached house or 4 m on a detached house, a loft conversion adding up to 40 m³ (terrace) or 50 m³ (semi or detached), and garden outbuildings up to 2.5 m high near a boundary. If any part of the project breaks a limit, or the property is a flat, listed, in a conservation area or has had its rights removed, you need planning permission instead.

Permitted development rights are a national grant of planning permission, given in advance, for specific types of building work. Stay inside the limits and you do not need to apply to the council. Step outside them by even 100 millimetres and you need full planning permission for the whole project, not just the part that went over.

That all-or-nothing quality is why permitted development is worth understanding properly. You are either inside the box or outside it. This guide shows how to tell, with the main limits for each type of project and the situations where the rights do not apply at all. It covers houses in England; Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules.

Key takeaways

  • Permitted development is not a loophole. It is planning permission that already exists, with strict numerical limits.
  • Flats and maisonettes have no householder permitted development rights.
  • Conservation areas, listed buildings, Article 4 directions and estate planning conditions can remove or reduce the rights.
  • Previous owners' extensions count against your allowance, because limits are measured from the "original" house.
  • Permitted development removes the need for planning permission only. Building regulations still apply.
  • A lawful development certificate is the safest proof that your project is permitted development.

What is permitted development?

Permitted development rights come from the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015. Schedule 2, Part 1 of that order lists the work householders can do without applying, split into classes: Class A for extensions, Class B for roof enlargements, Class C for other roof alterations such as rooflights, Class D for porches, Class E for outbuildings and Class F for hard surfaces. Each class has its own limits and conditions, and the work has to meet every one of them.

The limits are measured against the original house, meaning the house as it stood on 1 July 1948, or as first built if it was built later. Extensions added by previous owners count against your allowance even if you never saw them being built.

What can you build without planning permission?

Here are the main householder permitted development limits in one place. Every condition in a class must be met, so treat this as a first check rather than a final answer.

Across all extensions and outbuildings sits the 50 per cent rule: buildings added to the original house must not cover more than half of the land around it. Balconies, verandas and raised platforms over 0.3 m high are never permitted development.

Prior approval for larger rear extensions

The larger home extension scheme roughly doubles the single storey rear depth to 6 metres for attached houses and 8 metres for detached houses. It is not automatic. You notify the council with drawings, the council consults your adjoining neighbours, and if anyone objects it decides whether the impact on their amenity is acceptable. The council has 42 days to decide, and if it does not, the extension can go ahead. It is quicker and cheaper than a full application but not guaranteed, and it is not available in conservation areas or other designated land.

When permitted development rights do not exist

This is where most of the trouble lives. Any one of these is enough to remove or restrict the rights:

  • Flats and maisonettes have no householder permitted development rights. Every external alteration needs an application. Our flat conversion drawings page covers that route.
  • Listed buildings need listed building consent for most work, internal as well as external.
  • Conservation areas, National Parks, National Landscapes (formerly AONBs), World Heritage Sites and the Broads lose side extensions, roof extensions and the larger home extension route, and cladding is restricted.
  • Article 4 directions let a council withdraw specific rights across an area. They are widely used for HMO conversions and in some places for front extensions, roof changes and windows.
  • Planning conditions on newer estates often remove permitted development rights as part of the original consent. This is very common on estates built in the last thirty years and easy to miss.
  • Previous extensions that have already used up the depth, volume or 50 per cent allowance.
  • Houses created by change of use under certain other permitted development routes, such as offices converted to homes, do not get householder rights.
To check whether rights have been removed, look at your council's online planning map for designations and Article 4 areas, search the planning history for your address and your estate, and check your title deeds for restrictive covenants. A condition removing rights sits in the estate's original decision notice, sometimes decades old, and will not appear on any map.

What is an Article 4 direction?

An Article 4 direction is a council order that removes specific permitted development rights in a defined area, so work that would normally be allowed needs planning permission there. Councils use them to protect the character of an area or control particular uses. The most common examples are converting houses into small HMOs, which many university cities control, and alterations to front elevations in conservation areas such as replacing windows or doors. Article 4 directions are not retrospective, so work lawfully completed before one came in is unaffected. If you are converting to an HMO, our HMO planning permission guide and HMO floor plans guide explain how this works in practice.

Permitted development does not mean unregulated

This misconception causes the most expensive problems. Permitted development removes the need for planning permission. It has no effect on:

  • Building regulations approval, which is needed for almost all extension and conversion work whatever the planning route. See building regulations drawings explained and planning permission vs building regulations.
  • The Party Wall etc. Act 1996, when building on or near a shared boundary.
  • Restrictive covenants in your title deeds, a private legal matter separate from planning.
  • Protected trees and hedgerows, which need separate consent to fell or prune.
  • Highway consent for dropped kerbs and work on the public footway.

Why get a lawful development certificate?

A lawful development certificate (LDC), also called a certificate of lawful development, is a formal decision from the council confirming that your proposed work is lawful without planning permission. It is optional, and plenty of people build under permitted development without one. It is still usually worth having:

  • It settles any later dispute with the council about whether the work was allowed.
  • Buyers' solicitors increasingly ask for proof that work was lawful, and without a certificate the burden falls on you when you sell.
  • The fee is lower than a full householder application.

The catch is that an LDC needs accurate, fully dimensioned drawings, because the council is certifying specific measurements. Our lawful development certificate guide covers the process in full, and our house extension drawings and loft conversion drawings services include LDC drawing sets.

How to decide which route you are on

  1. Establish the property type. If it is a flat or maisonette, you need planning permission.
  2. Check designations. Conservation area, listed status, National Park, National Landscape, Article 4 direction. The council's online planning map shows most of these.
  3. Check the planning history of the property and the estate for conditions removing permitted development rights.
  4. Measure what already exists. Previous extensions and loft enlargements count against your allowance.
  5. Measure the proposal accurately against the limits: depth, height, eaves height, boundary distance, width, volume and coverage.
  6. Choose the route. Inside every limit: build under permitted development, ideally with an LDC. Larger rear extension: prior approval. Outside the limits or uncertain: a householder planning application.

Steps two and three are where most people get caught, because the information is scattered across the planning map, the property's history and the title deeds. It is a short piece of work that routinely saves months, and it is the first thing we check before drawing any extension. Frequently asked questions

What is permitted development?

Permitted development is planning permission granted in advance by national law for certain work to houses, such as modest extensions, loft conversions, porches and outbuildings. If the work meets every limit and condition, no planning application is needed.

What is the difference between permitted development and planning permission?

Permitted development is automatic permission for work within fixed national limits, with no application needed. Planning permission is granted by the council after assessing a specific application on its design and impact, which allows larger or different projects.

How far can I extend without planning permission?

A single storey rear extension can usually go 3 metres from the original rear wall on a semi-detached or terraced house and 4 metres on a detached house. Through prior approval this rises to 6 and 8 metres, subject to neighbour consultation.

Do I need planning permission for a garden room?

Usually not, if it is single storey, behind the front of the house, no more than 2.5 metres high within 2 metres of a boundary, and used for purposes incidental to the house rather than as a bedroom or separate home. Listed buildings and some designated areas have tighter rules.

Do I need planning permission for a porch?

Not if the porch has a ground floor area of no more than 3 square metres, is no higher than 3 metres and is at least 2 metres from any boundary with a road.

Do flats have permitted development rights?

No. Flats and maisonettes do not have householder permitted development rights, so external alterations and extensions need planning permission.

How do I know if my permitted development rights have been removed?

Check the council's planning map for conservation areas and Article 4 directions, search the planning history of your property and estate for conditions removing the rights, and ask your council if unsure. Many newer estates removed them in the original permission.

Does permitted development need building regulations?

Yes. Permitted development only removes the need for planning permission. Extensions, loft conversions and most structural work still need building regulations approval.
