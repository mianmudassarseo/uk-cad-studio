Planning Drawings for a House Extension: What Your Extension Plans Must Include

Most delayed extension applications are not refused on design. They are rejected at validation because a drawing or form is missing. Here is every plan a householder application needs, at the right scale, with typical costs.

House extensions Updated 28 October 2026 Published 18 February 2026 14 min read
Quick answer

A householder planning application for a house extension normally needs seven drawings: a location plan at 1:1250 or 1:2500 with the site edged red, a block plan at 1:500 or 1:200, existing and proposed floor plans, existing and proposed elevations, and a roof plan, usually at 1:50 or 1:100. A section is often added for sloping sites or complex roofs. Every drawing needs a stated metric scale, a scale bar, a north point on plans and a drawing number. Planning-stage drawings for a simple single storey extension typically cost around £1,000 to £2,500.

There is a difference between an application that gets refused and one that gets rejected, and it matters more than most homeowners realise. Refusal means a planning officer assessed your scheme and decided it was unacceptable. Rejection at validation means nobody assessed anything: the submission was incomplete and went back before the eight week clock even started.

Validation failures are entirely avoidable, and they are the most common reason a straightforward extension takes four months instead of two. This guide sets out exactly which extension plans a householder application needs, how each one should be drawn, and what else goes in the submission. It covers England.

Key takeaways

  • Most extension applications need seven drawings, plus a section on sloping or complex sites.
  • The roof plan is the drawing most often missing from extension applications.
  • Existing and proposed drawings must be at the same scale so the officer can compare them.
  • The CIL form and the right ownership certificate catch out many otherwise perfect applications.
  • Planning drawings show what the extension looks like. A separate building regulations package shows how it is built.
  • You do not need an architect by law, but you do need accurate, properly scaled drawings.

What each extension plan must include

1. Location plan

An Ordnance Survey based plan at 1:1250 (or 1:2500 for rural sites) showing the property in its wider street context. The application site is edged in red, including the route to the nearest road, and any other land you own nearby is edged in blue. The plan should show at least two named roads and enough surrounding buildings to make the site identifiable, with a north point and a stated scale. Location plans must be bought from a licensed map supplier; a printout from a free online map is not acceptable.

2. Block or site plan

A closer view at 1:500 or 1:200 showing your boundaries, the existing building, the proposed extension footprint and its distance to each boundary and neighbouring building. It should also show north, trees near the work, hard surfacing, parking and, where relevant, drainage.

3. Existing floor plans

Every floor of the house as it stands, at 1:50 or 1:100, with room names and overall dimensions. This is the baseline the officer compares your proposal against, so it needs to be accurate. Drawings built on guessed dimensions undermine everything drawn on top of them.

4. Proposed floor plans

The same floors showing the extension, at the same scale as the existing plans. New walls should be clearly distinguished from retained structure, usually by colour or hatching, and every room labelled.

5. Existing elevations

Every elevation of the house as built: usually four, or two for a mid-terrace. Each shows windows, doors, materials, roof form and ground levels.

6. Proposed elevations

Every elevation the extension changes, with materials annotated (for example "facing brick to match existing", "grey aluminium bifolds"). Include elevations that face a neighbour, not just the street. Officers assess overlooking and overshadowing from these.

7. Roof plan

Frequently forgotten and frequently requested. A plan view of the existing and proposed roof showing pitches, ridges, valleys, hips, parapets and rooflights. On a wrap-around or L shaped extension, the roof plan is often the drawing that shows whether the design actually works.

8. Sections, where required

Not always mandatory, but often requested where the site slopes, the roof is complex, or the height near a boundary needs explaining. If your garden falls away or the extension steps down, include one.

The rules every planning drawing must follow

  • A stated metric scale on every drawing. "Not to scale" or "do not scale from this drawing" is not acceptable on a planning submission.
  • A scale bar as well as the written scale, so the drawing stays measurable if printed at another size.
  • A north point on all plan views.
  • A unique drawing number and revision on every sheet, so the decision notice can list the approved drawings.
  • Existing and proposed at matching scales so they can be compared directly.
  • The right paper size, usually A3 or A1. A 1:50 plan squeezed onto A4 is not legible.
  • Accurate dimensions from a measured survey or verified measurements, never from marketing floor plans.
A decision notice grants permission for specific numbered drawings. If you later build something different from those drawings, you have not built what was approved. That is why drawing numbers and revisions matter long after the application.

What else goes in a householder planning application

  • Application form, usually submitted online through the Planning Portal.
  • The planning fee. Householder fees are set nationally and rise periodically, so check the current figure on the Planning Portal fee calculator.
  • Ownership certificate. Certificate A if you own all the land. Certificate B if any part of the work, including foundations, sits on land owned by someone else, such as a neighbour on the boundary, in which case you must serve them notice first.
  • CIL form. Most councils ask for the Community Infrastructure Levy additional information form even though householder extensions under 100 m² are normally exempt. A missing CIL form is a very common validation delay.
  • Heritage statement or design and access statement in conservation areas or for listed buildings.
  • Specialist reports where relevant: a flood risk assessment in a flood zone, a tree survey where the work is near protected trees, or a bat survey for some roof work.

The most common validation failures

  1. Missing roof plan, the most requested absent drawing on extension applications.
  2. Location plan not OS-based or at the wrong scale.
  3. An elevation left out because it faces a neighbour.
  4. No scale bar, or one that no longer matches after resizing.
  5. Existing and proposed at different scales.
  6. Red line not reaching the road, or wrong ownership certificate.
  7. No CIL form where the council's validation list asks for one.

How a householder application works

  1. Submit the drawings, form, fee and supporting documents online.
  2. Validation. The council checks everything required is there. Incomplete applications are returned.
  3. Consultation. Neighbours are notified and have 21 days to comment, and a site notice may be posted.
  4. Assessment. The case officer reviews the drawings against local policy and may ask for amendments.
  5. Decision. Councils aim to decide householder applications within 8 weeks of validation.

Do you always need planning permission for an extension?

No. Many single storey rear extensions fall within permitted development, so no application is needed. It depends on the depth, height and position of the extension, whether the house is detached or attached, and whether the property is a flat, listed, in a conservation area or covered by an Article 4 direction. Our guide to permitted development vs planning permission sets out the limits, and building an extension without planning permission covers the checks.

Even under permitted development, a lawful development certificate is usually worth having. It costs less than a full application, it confirms in writing that the work is lawful, and buyers' solicitors increasingly ask for it. It needs the same accurate, scaled drawings as a planning application.

Do you need an architect for extension plans?

No. There is no legal requirement to use an architect for planning drawings. "Architect" is a protected title for people registered with the Architects Registration Board, but architectural designers, technologists and CAD technicians can prepare planning drawings too. What matters is accurate measurements, correct scales and experience of what your council expects. For a comparison of fees, read how much an architect costs for a house extension.

How much do planning drawings for an extension cost?

For a simple single storey extension, published 2026 market guides typically put planning-stage drawing fees at around £1,000 to £2,500. Larger, two storey or more complex schemes cost more, and building regulations drawings are a separate, larger fee. The main cost drivers are whether a measured survey is needed, the size and complexity of the extension, the number of design options and revisions, and any heritage or specialist reports. See our architectural drawings cost guide and house extension cost guide for 2026 for the full budget.

After planning: building regulations drawings

Planning drawings are not construction drawings. They show what the extension will look like and where it sits, but say almost nothing about how it is built. Once permission is granted you need a separate building regulations package covering structure, insulation, ventilation, drainage and fire safety. Read planning drawings vs building regulations drawings, building regulations drawings explained and building regulations for a house extension, or see our building regulations drawings service. Budgeting for both stages from the start is one of the most reliable ways to keep an extension on programme.

Frequently asked questions

What drawings do I need for a house extension planning application?

Usually a location plan, a block plan, existing and proposed floor plans, existing and proposed elevations, and a roof plan. A section is often added for sloping sites or complex roofs. Your council's local validation list may ask for more.

What scale should extension plans be drawn at?

Floor plans and elevations are normally 1:50 or 1:100, the block plan 1:500 or 1:200, and the location plan 1:1250 or 1:2500. Every drawing needs the scale stated and a scale bar.

What is the difference between a location plan and a block plan?

A location plan at 1:1250 shows where the property is in the street, with the site edged red. A block plan at 1:500 or 1:200 shows the property, its boundaries, the extension footprint and nearby buildings in more detail.

How much do planning drawings for an extension cost?

For a simple single storey extension, planning-stage drawings typically cost around £1,000 to £2,500 in 2026. Larger schemes cost more, and building regulations drawings are a separate fee.

Do I need an architect to draw extension plans?

No. There is no legal requirement to use an architect. Architectural designers and technicians can prepare planning drawings, as long as they are accurate, properly scaled and meet the council's validation requirements.

Do I need a CIL form for an extension?

Many councils ask for the CIL additional information form with householder applications, even though extensions under 100 square metres are normally exempt from the levy. Leaving it out is a common cause of validation delays.

How long does planning permission for an extension take?

Councils aim to decide householder applications within 8 weeks of validation. Missing drawings or forms can delay validation and add weeks before that clock starts.

Can I use planning drawings to build the extension?

No. Planning drawings show appearance and layout only. You also need building regulations drawings with construction details, structural information and specifications before building.
