What planning drawings do you need for a house extension?

Most refused extension applications are not refused on design. They are rejected at validation, before an officer has even looked at the scheme. Here is exactly what a householder application needs.

House extensions 18 February 2026 8 min read

There is a difference between an application that gets refused and one that gets rejected, and it matters more than most homeowners realise. Refusal means a planning officer assessed your scheme and decided it was unacceptable. Rejection at validation means nobody assessed anything — the submission was incomplete, and it was sent back before the eight-week clock even started.

Validation failures are entirely avoidable, and they are the single most common reason a straightforward extension takes four months instead of two. This is what a householder planning application actually needs.

The drawings every householder application requires

Whatever your local authority, a householder application for an extension will need the following as a minimum. Some councils ask for more; almost none ask for less.

1. Location plan at 1:1250

An Ordnance Survey based plan showing the property in its wider street context, with the application site outlined in red. Any other land you own adjoining the site should be outlined in blue. The plan must show at least two named roads and enough surrounding buildings to make the site identifiable, and it needs a north point and a stated scale.

2. Site or block plan at 1:200 or 1:500

A closer view showing your property's boundaries, the existing building footprint, the proposed extension footprint, and the relationship to neighbouring buildings. It should show the direction of north, existing trees, hard surfacing, and where relevant the position of drainage and parking.

3. Existing floor plans

Every floor of the property as it currently stands, drawn to a standard metric scale — normally 1:50 or 1:100 — with room names and overall dimensions. This is the baseline the officer compares your proposal against, and getting it wrong undermines everything drawn on top of it.

4. Proposed floor plans

The same floors showing the extension, at the same scale as the existing plans so the two can be compared directly. New walls should be clearly distinguished from retained structure, and room uses labelled.

5. Existing elevations

All elevations of the property as built — usually four, though a mid-terrace will have two. Each needs to show window and door positions, materials, roof form and ground levels.

6. Proposed elevations

All affected elevations showing the extension in place, with materials annotated. If the extension changes the appearance of an elevation at all, that elevation must be included, including any that face a neighbour rather than the street.

7. Roof plan

Frequently forgotten, frequently requested. A plan view of the existing and proposed roof showing pitches, ridges, valleys, hips and rooflights. On a wrap-around extension the roof plan is often the drawing that reveals whether the design actually works.

8. Sections, where required

Not always mandatory for a householder application, but frequently requested where the site slopes, where the extension has a complex roof, or where the relationship to a boundary needs explaining. Many officers will ask for one if it is missing and the site is anything other than flat.

The rules the drawings must follow

  • A stated metric scale on every drawing. "Not to scale" or "do not scale from this drawing" is not acceptable on a planning submission.
  • A scale bar as well as the written scale, so the drawing remains measurable if it is printed at a different size.
  • A north point on all plan views.
  • A unique drawing number and revision on every sheet, so the council can reference the approved drawings in the decision notice.
  • Existing and proposed at matching scales so they can be directly compared.
  • Correct paper size. Most authorities want A3 or A1 depending on scale; a 1:50 plan squeezed onto A4 will not be legible.
A decision notice grants permission for specific numbered drawings. If you later build something that differs from those drawings, you have not built what was approved — which is why drawing numbers and revisions matter far beyond the application itself.

What else goes in the submission

Alongside the drawings, a householder application normally requires the standard application form, the correct fee, ownership certificates, and in many cases a completed Community Infrastructure Levy form even where no levy is payable. That CIL form catches a lot of people out — it is a frequent cause of validation delay on applications that were otherwise perfect.

In a conservation area, or where the building is listed, you will usually also need a heritage statement explaining how the proposal responds to the significance of the building and area. Some authorities require a design and access statement for householder work in designated areas.

The five most common validation failures

  1. Missing roof plan. The most-requested absent drawing on extension applications.
  2. Location plan not OS-based, or at the wrong scale. A screenshot from an online map is not a location plan.
  3. An elevation left out because it faces a neighbour and the applicant assumed nobody would care. Officers care.
  4. No scale bar, or a scale bar that does not match the stated scale after the drawing was resized.
  5. Existing and proposed drawn at different scales, making direct comparison impossible.

Do you always need planning permission?

No. A substantial proportion of single-storey rear extensions fall within permitted development rights, which means no planning application at all. The allowances depend on whether the house is detached or attached, the height and depth of the extension, its proximity to boundaries, and whether the property is a flat, is listed, or sits in a conservation area or Article 4 area.

Even where permitted development applies, it is usually worth applying for a Lawful Development Certificate. It costs considerably less than a full application, and it gives you written confirmation from the council that the works are lawful — which conveyancing solicitors increasingly ask for when you sell.

And after planning?

Planning drawings are not construction drawings. They show what the building will look like and where it will sit; they say almost nothing about how it is built. Once permission is granted you will need a separate building regulations package covering structure, insulation, ventilation, drainage, fire safety and accessibility.

Treating those as two distinct pieces of work — and budgeting for both — is one of the more reliable ways to keep an extension project on programme.
