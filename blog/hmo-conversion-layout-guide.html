HMO conversion layouts: a practical design guide

An HMO layout is not a design problem with regulatory constraints attached. The regulations are the design problem. Here is how to work from them outwards.

HMO design 21 March 2026 10 min read

The most expensive mistake in HMO conversion is designing the layout first and checking the standards afterwards. By the time you discover that room four is 6.2 square metres rather than 6.51, the stud walls are up and the loss is a whole letting room for the life of the building.

Good HMO design runs the other way. Start with the standards, work out how many compliant rooms the building can actually carry, and only then think about how the space feels.

Step one: establish the planning position

Before any measuring, find out whether you can legally change the use at all.

A small HMO for three to six unrelated people falls into use class C4, and changing from a family dwelling (C3) to C4 is permitted development under national rules. But a large and growing number of councils have made Article 4 directions removing that right, often across whole wards or entire local authority areas — particularly in university cities. Where an Article 4 direction applies, you need full planning permission.

An HMO for seven or more occupants is sui generis — outside the use class system entirely — and always requires planning permission, everywhere, with no exceptions.

Many councils also apply a concentration policy, refusing applications where the proportion of existing HMOs within a defined radius already exceeds a threshold. That check is worth doing before you exchange on a property, not after.

Step two: the statutory room sizes

Mandatory HMO licensing in England applies where a property is occupied by five or more people forming two or more households. The national licence conditions set minimum sleeping room floor areas:

  • 6.51 m² for one person aged over 10
  • 10.22 m² for two people aged over 10
  • 4.64 m² for one child under 10

Critically, floor area under a ceiling height of 1.5 metres does not count toward the total. On a loft room with sloping ceilings, this can remove a third of the apparent floor area, and it is the single most common reason a room fails on measurement.

These are national minimums, not targets. Most councils operate additional or selective licensing schemes with higher standards — larger rooms, minimum kitchen worktop and storage per person, and specific WC and bathroom ratios. Design to your council's published standards, not the national floor.

Step three: amenity ratios

Councils set requirements for how many sharers each kitchen and bathroom can serve. The exact figures vary by authority, but the shape of the requirement is consistent: a ratio of WCs, wash basins and baths or showers to occupants, plus minimum kitchen provision measured in worktop length, storage volume, sink units and cooking rings per person.

In practice this frequently determines the room count rather than the room sizes do. A six-bedroom house with one bathroom will usually fail regardless of how generous the bedrooms are, and adding a second bathroom means finding drainage — which is a plan question, decided early or decided expensively.

Step four: the fire strategy

This is where HMO layouts most often need real design thought rather than arithmetic. A converted HMO typically requires:

  • A protected escape route from every storey to a final exit, usually meaning the staircase and hallway are enclosed in fire-resisting construction.
  • Fire doors — commonly FD30S with intumescent strips and cold smoke seals — to every habitable room opening onto that route, fitted with self-closers.
  • Fire-resisting floors and ceilings between storeys, which in an older property usually means upgrading existing lath and plaster or thin plasterboard ceilings.
  • An interlinked alarm system of a grade and category appropriate to the size and layout — commonly a Grade A or D system with LD2 or LD1 coverage depending on the property.
  • Emergency lighting on longer or more complex escape routes.
  • Fire-resisting construction to the kitchen, which is statistically where the fire starts.

The design consequence is that you cannot put a bedroom door where it creates an inner room — a room whose only escape is through another room. Inner rooms are one of the most common reasons a layout has to be redrawn, and they are easy to create accidentally when subdividing a large reception room.

Practical layout principles

Do not create inner rooms

Every bedroom needs a door opening directly onto the protected route. Subdividing a through-lounge into two rooms where the back one opens off the front one will fail.

Follow the existing drainage

En-suites are attractive commercially and expensive structurally. Cluster them vertically above one another and above the existing soil stack wherever the plan allows. A bathroom on the opposite side of the building from the stack means a long run at shallow fall, boxed-in pipework and often a pumped solution.

Protect the communal space

It is tempting to convert the last reception room into a seventh bedroom. Many councils require a communal living space in addition to the kitchen for HMOs above a certain occupancy, and even where they do not, a property with nowhere to sit outside your bedroom lets less well and turns over faster.

Measure ceiling heights before you count rooms

Especially in loft rooms and converted cellars. The 1.5 metre rule and the general adequacy of head height will decide whether a room counts at all.

Think about sound

Not always a statutory requirement in a converted HMO in the way it is between separate flats, but it is the leading cause of tenant complaints and turnover. Bedroom-to-bedroom partitions built with a mineral wool quilt and two layers of dense board cost very little more and materially improve retention.

What to draw

A complete HMO drawing package includes an existing measured survey, a proposed layout with every room annotated with its net usable floor area, an amenity schedule showing the provision against the applicable standard, and a fire strategy drawing marking escape routes, compartmentation lines, fire door ratings, alarm positions and emergency lighting.

Annotating the floor areas directly on the plan is not a formality. It lets a licensing officer verify compliance without a scale rule, and it materially reduces the chance of a query holding up your licence.
