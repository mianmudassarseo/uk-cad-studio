HMO Floor Plans: A Practical Conversion Layout Guide for UK Landlords

Room sizes, kitchen and bathroom ratios, fire strategy, building regulations and use class rules, plus a worked six-bedroom HMO floor plan example you can check your own property against.

HMO design Updated 28 October 2026 Published 21 March 2026 14 min read
Quick answer

To pass licensing in England, each HMO bedroom for one adult needs at least 6.51 m² of floor space with a ceiling height of 1.5 m or more (10.22 m² for two adults). You also need enough kitchens and bathrooms for the number of sharers under your council's amenity standards, a protected escape route with FD30S fire doors, and a suitable interlinked fire alarm. Three to six sharers is use class C4; seven or more is sui generis and always needs planning permission.

The quickest way to lose money on an HMO conversion is to draw the layout first and check the standards later. By the time you find out that room four is 6.2 square metres rather than 6.51, the stud walls are up and you have lost a letting room for the life of the building.

A good HMO floor plan works the other way round. Start from the licensing, planning and fire rules, work out how many compliant rooms the building can carry, and only then think about how the space feels to live in.

Key takeaways

  • Check the planning position (C4, sui generis, Article 4) before you buy or measure anything.
  • Measure rooms at the 1.5 m ceiling line. Loft rooms fail on this more than anything else.
  • Bathroom and kitchen ratios often decide the room count, so plan drainage early.
  • Every bedroom must open directly onto the protected escape route. Inner rooms get rejected.
  • Building regulations and the HMO licence are separate approvals and both need the same fire strategy.
  • Your council's local standards usually sit above the national minimums. Design to the higher figure.

Step one: check the HMO use class and planning position

Before anyone gets the tape measure out, find out whether you can legally change the use of the building at all. The answer depends on how many people will live there and whether your council has made an Article 4 direction.

HMO use classes in England
OccupancyUse classPlanning permission needed?
Single household (family home)C3 dwellinghouseNo change of use
3 to 6 unrelated people sharing facilitiesC4 small HMONo, it is permitted development, unless an Article 4 direction applies
7 or more unrelated peopleSui generis large HMOYes, always, in every council area

Many councils, especially in university cities, have used Article 4 directions to remove the C3 to C4 permitted development right across whole wards or the entire borough. Where one applies you need a full planning application, and a lot of those councils also run a concentration policy that refuses new HMOs once a set share of homes within a given radius are already HMOs. Do that check before you exchange contracts.

If a property already operates as a small HMO, a lawful development certificate can confirm the existing use. For the full application route, timings and what councils look for, read our guide to HMO planning permission and the explainer on permitted development versus planning permission.

Step two: design to the statutory HMO room sizes

Mandatory HMO licensing in England applies when five or more people forming two or more households live in a property. Since 1 October 2018 the national licence conditions have set minimum floor areas for rooms used for sleeping.

National minimum HMO bedroom sizes (England)
Who sleeps in the roomMinimum floor area
One person aged over 106.51 m²
Two people aged over 1010.22 m²
One child under 104.64 m²
Any room under 4.64 m²Cannot be used for sleeping

The rule that catches most layouts out is the ceiling height one. Any floor area where the ceiling is lower than 1.5 metres does not count. In a loft room with sloping ceilings that can remove a third of what looks like usable floor, and a room you counted as a bedroom on the estate agent's plan turns into a store.

These are national minimums, not targets. Most councils run additional or selective licensing with their own standards, often asking for bigger rooms (8 m² or more for a single bedroom without a separate lounge is common), minimum kitchen worktop and storage per person, and set WC and bathroom ratios. Design to your council's published figure.

If you are adding rooms in the roof, our loft conversion drawings service and the guide to loft conversion types explain how a dormer changes the usable area.

Step three: work out the kitchen and bathroom ratios

Every council sets out how many sharers each kitchen, bathroom and WC can serve. The numbers vary, but most authorities start from a similar benchmark.

Typical council amenity benchmark (always confirm your local standard)
Number of sharersBathroomsWCs
1 to 411 (can be in the bathroom)
511 separate WC with wash basin
6 to 1022 (one can be in a bathroom)
11 to 1533 (one can be in a bathroom)

Kitchens are usually measured in sinks, cooking rings, ovens, worktop length and fridge and cupboard space per person, with one full set of facilities commonly expected per five sharers. An en-suite counts towards the bathroom total, though some councils only count it for the person whose room it serves.

In practice the bathroom count decides the room count more often than room sizes do. A six-bedroom house with one bathroom will fail however generous the bedrooms are, and adding a second bathroom means finding a drainage route. That is a floor plan decision, and it is far cheaper to make on paper than on site.

Step four: build the fire strategy into the layout

Fire safety is where an HMO floor plan needs real design thinking rather than arithmetic. Councils assess HMOs against the LACORS national guidance on fire safety in existing housing, and building control looks at the same measures. A converted HMO typically needs:

  • A protected escape route from every storey to a final exit, usually the staircase and hallway enclosed in 30-minute fire-resisting construction.
  • Fire doors, normally FD30S with intumescent strips and cold smoke seals, on every room opening onto that route, fitted with self-closers.
  • Fire-resisting floors and ceilings between storeys. In older houses this often means upgrading lath and plaster or thin plasterboard.
  • An interlinked fire alarm to BS 5839-6. Smaller shared houses commonly get a Grade D system with LD2 coverage, while larger or bedsit-style HMOs often need a Grade A panel system with LD2 or LD1 coverage.
  • Emergency lighting on longer or more complex escape routes.
  • A fire-separated kitchen, since that is where most fires start.

The big layout consequence is that you cannot create an inner room, which is a room whose only way out is through another room. Inner rooms are one of the most common reasons an HMO layout has to be redrawn, and they are easy to create by accident when splitting a large through-lounge.

Building regulations for HMO conversion

Planning and licensing do not cover the construction itself. The work involved in turning a house into an HMO (new partitions, fire doors and ceilings, alarm wiring, extra bathrooms, drainage and electrics) is building work that needs approval from building control, either the council or a registered building control approver. You can read how the two systems differ in planning permission versus building regulations.

Approved Documents most relevant to an HMO conversion
Approved DocumentWhat it covers in an HMO
Part B (fire safety)Escape routes, fire doors, compartment floors and walls, alarms
Part E (sound)Sound insulation for new partitions and floors, especially where rooms are treated as rooms for residential purposes
Part F (ventilation)Extract fans to kitchens, bathrooms and en-suites, background ventilation to bedrooms
Part H (drainage)New soil and waste runs for added bathrooms and kitchens
Part L (energy)Thermal upgrades where walls, roofs or windows are renovated
Part P (electrical safety)New circuits, consumer unit and alarm wiring

Since October 2023, anyone commissioning building work in England also takes on client dutyholder responsibilities under the Building Safety Act regime, which means making sure the designer and builder are competent and the work is planned to comply. A clear building regulations drawing package is the simplest way to show that, and our article on building regulations drawings explained walks through what the sheets contain.

Two lessons come out of this schedule. The loft room only works because the dormer lifts enough floor above 1.5 metres, and Bedroom 5 passes or fails purely on which council the house sits in. In a borough with an 8 m² local standard the fix is usually to borrow space from the landing or drop to five letting rooms, and it is better to know that before you buy than after the stud walls go up.

For a five-bed version of the same house, the separate ground floor WC still matters because five sharers usually trigger the separate WC requirement. If you want this kind of schedule drawn for your own property, our HMO conversion drawings service annotates every room this way.

Practical HMO layout principles

Give every bedroom a door onto the protected route

Splitting a through-lounge so that the back room opens off the front one creates an inner room and will fail. Add a short corridor or move the door.

Stack bathrooms over the existing drainage

En-suites help the rent but cost money in pipework. Cluster them above one another and close to the existing soil stack. A bathroom on the far side of the house means long runs at shallow falls, boxing in and often a pumped macerator.

Keep a communal living space

Turning the last reception room into a seventh bedroom is tempting. Many councils require a shared living area as well as the kitchen above a certain occupancy, and even where they do not, a house with nowhere to sit outside the bedrooms lets for less and turns over faster.

Measure ceiling heights before counting rooms

This matters most in lofts and converted cellars. The 1.5 metre rule and the general head height decide whether a room counts at all.

Plan for sound between bedrooms

Noise is the leading cause of tenant complaints. Bedroom partitions with mineral wool and two layers of acoustic board cost very little more than a basic stud wall and keep tenants longer.

Think about converting to flats instead

On some larger houses, self-contained units give a better return or face fewer local restrictions. Our flat conversion drawings page covers that route.

Local HMO rules: Newcastle upon Tyne and Havering

National rules are only half the picture. Two areas we get frequent questions about show how much the local position can change a project.

HMO conversions in Newcastle upon Tyne

Newcastle City Council has made several Article 4 directions for HMOs. The first, covering parts of Jesmond and Heaton, came into force in November 2011, with further directions for High West Jesmond and North Jesmond and for St Gabriel's Estate in Heaton. Inside those areas a house to small HMO change needs planning permission. Outside them a C3 to C4 change is still permitted development, and the council notes that moving from a small HMO to one for more than six people will normally need permission. Most HMOs in the city also need a licence under the Housing Act. Check the council's interactive Article 4 map for your exact address, and see our Newcastle upon Tyne drawing services page for local guidance.

Havering Council HMO rules

The London Borough of Havering removed permitted development rights for C4 HMOs through an Article 4 direction that took effect on 13 July 2016, so converting a family house to an HMO there needs planning permission. Havering also started a new additional HMO licensing scheme and a selective licensing scheme on 18 March 2026, which brings many smaller HMOs into licensing. If you are working in east London, our London drawing services page explains how we handle borough-specific standards.

Other university cities with tight HMO controls include Leeds, Nottingham, Sheffield and Manchester.

What HMO floor plans need to show

A complete HMO drawing package for licensing, planning and building control usually includes:

  1. Measured survey of the existing building with accurate room areas and ceiling heights.
  2. Proposed HMO floor plans with every room labelled by use, occupancy and net usable area in m².
  3. Amenity schedule comparing kitchens, bathrooms and WCs with the council standard.
  4. Fire strategy plan showing escape routes, compartment lines, fire door ratings, detector and alarm positions and emergency lighting.
  5. Planning drawings (location plan, existing and proposed plans and elevations) where Article 4 or sui generis status applies. See drawings needed for planning permission.
  6. Building regulations details covering fire-resisting construction, sound, ventilation and drainage. Our comparison of planning drawings and building regulations drawings explains the difference.

Writing the floor area straight onto each room is worth the effort. It lets the licensing officer check compliance without a scale rule and cuts down the queries that hold up a licence. For a sense of fees, see our architectural drawings cost guide for 2026.

Frequently asked questions

What should an HMO floor plan show?

Every room with its use and net usable floor area in square metres, the number of occupants per room, kitchen and bathroom provision, fire doors and ratings, the protected escape route, detector positions and any emergency lighting. Leave out floor area below 1.5 metres ceiling height.

What is the minimum room size in an HMO?

6.51 m² for one person over 10, 10.22 m² for two people over 10, and 4.64 m² for a child under 10, under the 2018 mandatory licence conditions in England. Floor under 1.5 metres ceiling height does not count, and many councils set higher local figures.

What use class is an HMO?

A small HMO for three to six unrelated people is use class C4. Seven or more is sui generis and always needs planning permission. A C3 to C4 change is permitted development unless the council has an Article 4 direction.

Do I need building regulations approval for an HMO conversion?

In most cases yes. New partitions, fire doors and ceilings, alarm systems, bathrooms, drainage and electrics are all building work that needs building control sign-off, and the fire measures should match what the licensing officer expects.

How many bathrooms does a 6 bedroom HMO need?

The common benchmark for six to ten sharers is two bathrooms and two WCs, with one WC allowed inside a bathroom. Your council's amenity standards set the final figure.

Do I need planning permission for an HMO in Newcastle upon Tyne?

Only inside the city's Article 4 areas, which include parts of Jesmond and Heaton, or for any HMO with seven or more occupants. Elsewhere a C3 to C4 change is permitted development.

What are Havering Council's HMO rules?

An Article 4 direction since 13 July 2016 means C3 to C4 conversions need planning permission, and a new additional HMO licensing scheme started on 18 March 2026.

Can a loft room count as an HMO bedroom?

Yes, if the area with at least 1.5 metres of headroom meets the minimum size and the room has a safe escape route. Measure at the 1.5 metre line before counting it.
