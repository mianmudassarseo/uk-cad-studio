Bungalow Loft Conversion: Cost, Planning and Layout Ideas

A bungalow loft conversion can add two bedrooms without losing garden. Here is how to tell if your roof is suitable, what each type costs in 2026, and the planning and building regulations rules.

Loft conversions Updated 12 October 2026 12 min read
Quick answer

Many bungalows can take a loft conversion, but it depends on the roof pitch and head height: you generally want 2.2 to 2.4 m from the top of the ceiling joists to the ridge. Published 2026 UK guides put a bungalow loft conversion at around £20,000 to £50,000 for a rooflight conversion, £30,000 to £60,000 for a dormer, £40,000 to £65,000 for a hip to gable and £55,000 to £80,000 for a mansard. Dormer and hip to gable conversions are often permitted development within 50 m³ for a detached or semi-detached bungalow, but raising the roof always needs planning permission. Building regulations always apply.

A bungalow loft conversion turns a single storey home into a chalet style house, often adding two bedrooms and a bathroom without losing any garden. It is a popular way to make room for a growing family or a live-in relative. But bungalows bring their own problems: shallow roofs, trussed rafters, and a staircase that has to take space from the only existing floor.

This guide covers whether your bungalow is suitable, the conversion types and 2026 costs, planning permission, building regulations and where to put the stairs. It covers England.

Key takeaways

  • Head height and roof structure decide whether a bungalow loft conversion is possible.
  • Shallow pitched bungalows usually need a dormer or hip to gable to gain usable height.
  • Typical 2026 costs range from about £20,000 to £80,000 depending on the conversion type.
  • Dormers and hip to gables are often permitted development, but raising the ridge always needs planning permission.
  • The staircase takes space from the ground floor, so plan it before anything else.
  • Because the new floor is usually low, escape windows can often be used for fire safety, alongside interlinked smoke alarms.

Is your bungalow suitable for a loft conversion?

Three checks decide most cases.

1. Head height

Measure from the top of the ceiling joists to the underside of the ridge. Around 2.2 to 2.4 m gives room for a new floor, insulation and comfortable standing height. Many 1960s and 1970s bungalows have shallow roofs and fall short, which means a dormer or hip to gable is needed to create full-height space.

2. Roof structure

Older bungalows often have traditional cut roofs with rafters and purlins, which are easier to open up. From the 1960s onwards many have trussed rafters, the W shaped timber frames that fill the loft. Trusses cannot simply be cut out. A structural engineer has to design a new structure, usually steel beams and new floor joists, which adds cost but is routine.

3. Staircase position

In a house, the new stair usually rises above the existing one. In a bungalow there is no existing stair, so it has to take space from a hallway, bedroom or living room. A straight flight needs a footprint of roughly 0.9 m by 3 m plus landings, and it must land under the highest part of the roof to get enough headroom.

Do you need planning permission for a bungalow loft conversion?

Often not. Roof enlargements to houses, including bungalows, can be permitted development if:

  • The extra volume is no more than 50 m³ for a detached or semi-detached bungalow, or 40 m³ for a terraced one, including any earlier roof additions.
  • Nothing goes higher than the existing ridge.
  • There is no dormer on the roof slope of the principal elevation facing a road.
  • Materials look similar to the existing roof.
  • Side windows are obscure glazed and non-opening below 1.7 m.
  • There are no balconies, and the property is not in a conservation area or other designated land.

You will need planning permission to raise the ridge, add a front dormer facing the road, build a mansard in most cases, or where permitted development rights have been removed, which is common on bungalow estates. Some councils also have policies to protect bungalows, so check local guidance early. See loft conversion planning permission and permitted development vs planning permission for the detail, and consider a lawful development certificate to confirm a permitted development scheme.

Building regulations for a bungalow loft conversion

Every loft conversion to a habitable room needs building regulations approval. The main points for a bungalow are:

Key building regulations points for bungalow lofts
AreaWhat is required
StructureNew floor joists and steel beams designed by an engineer, especially where trussed rafters are altered
StaircaseA fixed stair meeting Approved Document K. Headroom of 2 m, or 1.9 m at the centre falling to 1.8 m at the side for loft conversions
Fire safetyMains-wired interlinked smoke alarms. Where the new floor is no more than about 4.5 m above ground, habitable rooms can usually rely on an emergency escape window
Escape windowsClear openable area of at least 0.33 m², at least 450 mm high and wide, with the bottom of the opening no more than 1,100 mm above the floor
InsulationRoof, dormer walls and floor insulated to current Part L standards
Ventilation and soundBackground and extract ventilation, and sound insulation in the new floor

Bungalow loft conversion layout ideas

Two bedrooms and a shower room

A full width rear dormer on a typical detached bungalow can usually fit two bedrooms either side of a central landing, with a shower room in the dormer. The ground floor can then become open plan living with a guest bedroom.

Main bedroom suite

One large bedroom with an en-suite and dressing area, leaving the ground floor bedrooms for children or a home office.

Ground floor living for an older relative

Keeping a bedroom and bathroom on the ground floor makes the house suitable for later life or a live-in relative, while the family uses the new upper floor.

Stair placement

The best spot is usually where the hallway meets a bedroom that can be made slightly smaller, so the stair rises towards the ridge. Spiral stairs save space but are only acceptable for limited situations, so a straight or winder flight is normally used.

Should you raise the roof instead?

If the roof is very shallow, raising it can create a proper first floor with full height rooms. It always needs planning permission, it costs more and you usually need to move out during the work. Councils assess the effect on the street scene and neighbours, and in a street of bungalows a much taller house may be resisted. For a full second floor, it is worth comparing against a two storey extension or a single storey extension at ground level.

How a bungalow loft project runs

  1. Feasibility check of head height, roof structure, stair position and planning history.
  2. Measured survey of the roof void and ground floor.
  3. Design and planning route, either a lawful development certificate or a planning application.
  4. Structural design and building regulations drawings.
  5. Party wall notices for semi-detached and terraced bungalows where steels bear into the shared wall.
  6. Build, commonly 6 to 8 weeks on site for a straightforward conversion.

Frequently asked questions

Can you do a loft conversion on a bungalow?

Often yes, if there is enough head height, ideally 2.2 to 2.4 m from the ceiling joists to the ridge, and room for a staircase. Shallow roofs usually need a dormer or hip to gable, and very low roofs may need to be raised.

How much does a bungalow loft conversion cost?

Published 2026 UK guides suggest about £20,000 to £50,000 for a rooflight conversion, £30,000 to £60,000 for a dormer, £40,000 to £65,000 for a hip to gable and £55,000 to £80,000 for a mansard.

Do I need planning permission for a bungalow loft conversion?

Often not, if the extra roof volume is within 50 m³ for a detached or semi-detached bungalow, the ridge is not raised and there is no dormer facing the road. Raising the roof, front dormers and most mansards need planning permission.

Can you convert a loft with trussed rafters?

Yes, but the trusses cannot simply be removed. A structural engineer designs a new structure, usually steel beams and new joists, before any trusses are altered.

Where do the stairs go in a bungalow loft conversion?

Usually from the hallway or by taking part of a bedroom, rising towards the highest part of the roof so the stair has enough headroom. A straight flight needs roughly 0.9 m by 3 m plus landings.

Do bungalow loft conversions need fire doors?

Not always. Where the new floor is no more than about 4.5 m above ground, habitable rooms can usually rely on emergency escape windows plus mains-wired interlinked smoke alarms. Building control confirms the strategy for each design.

How long does a bungalow loft conversion take?

Design, approvals and structural work usually take a couple of months before building, then a straightforward conversion typically takes 6 to 8 weeks on site.
