Conservatory Planning Permission and Building Regulations Explained

Most conservatories are permitted development and exempt from building regulations, but both exemptions have conditions. Here is what applies to conservatories, orangeries and solid roof upgrades.

House extensions Updated 16 October 2026 11 min read
Quick answer

Most conservatories in England do not need planning permission, because they count as single storey extensions under permitted development: up to 3 m deep on a semi or terraced house and 4 m on a detached house, no more than 4 m high, eaves no higher than 3 m within 2 m of a boundary, and not at the front. Most are also exempt from building regulations if they are under 30 m², at ground level, separated from the house by external quality doors, and have their own heating controls. An orangery with a mostly solid roof, a solid replacement roof, or removing the doors to the house usually needs building regulations approval.

Conservatories have a reputation as the easy extension, and for good reason. Most are permitted development and exempt from building regulations. But both exemptions come with conditions, and many homeowners lose them without realising: by building too deep, choosing a solid roof, or knocking through to the kitchen a few years later.

This guide explains when a conservatory or orangery needs planning permission, when it needs building regulations, and what changes if you upgrade or open it up. It covers houses in England.

Key takeaways

  • Conservatories are treated as extensions for planning, so the normal permitted development limits apply.
  • The usual "materials similar to the house" condition does not apply to conservatories.
  • Building regulations exemption needs four things: under 30 m², ground level, thermal separation from the house, and independent heating.
  • Safety glazing and electrical work must always comply, even when the conservatory is exempt.
  • An orangery with a mainly solid roof is usually treated as an extension for building regulations.
  • Replacing a glass roof with a solid one, or removing the doors, normally needs building regulations approval.

Do you need planning permission for a conservatory?

Usually not. A conservatory is a single storey extension under Class A of the General Permitted Development Order 2015, so it is permitted development if it meets these limits:

Limits are measured from the original house, so an earlier extension or conservatory built by a previous owner counts against your allowance. For the full rules, see permitted development vs planning permission and single storey rear extension rules.

When a conservatory needs planning permission

  • It is deeper, taller or wider than the limits above.
  • The property is a flat or maisonette, which has no householder permitted development rights.
  • The house is listed, which usually needs listed building consent too.
  • It is a side conservatory in a conservation area, National Park, National Landscape, World Heritage Site or the Broads, where side extensions are not permitted development.
  • Permitted development rights have been removed by a condition or an Article 4 direction.
  • It would cover more than half the garden once other buildings are counted.

Does an orangery need planning permission?

For planning, an orangery is treated the same as a conservatory or any other single storey extension, so the same permitted development limits apply. The difference is in building regulations. Orangeries usually have more solid wall and a flat roof with a lantern, and if the roof is mostly solid it will not meet the definition of a conservatory, so building regulations approval is normally needed.

Do conservatories need building regulations?

A conservatory is exempt from most building regulations if all of these apply:

  1. It is built at ground level and has a floor area of no more than 30 m².
  2. It is separated from the house by external quality walls, doors or windows.
  3. It has an independent heating system with its own temperature and on/off controls, or no heating, rather than an extension of the house's heating.
  4. The glazing meets the safety glazing requirements of Part K and any fixed electrical work meets Part P.

For energy purposes, a conservatory is generally a structure with at least half its wall area and three quarters of its roof area glazed or translucent. Below that, it is treated as an ordinary extension.

Many conservatory frames and foundations were never designed for the weight of a tiled roof. Before upgrading to a solid roof, have the structure checked. Building control will want evidence that the walls, frame and foundations can carry the new load.

Opening a conservatory into the house

Removing the doors or wall between a conservatory and the kitchen is popular, but it changes the conservatory into a heated extension. Building regulations then apply, and the glazed walls and roof usually fall short of energy standards. The common solutions are fitting a high-performance solid or hybrid roof, upgrading the glazing, adding insulated walls, or demonstrating compliance through an energy calculation. Often it makes more sense to replace the conservatory with a properly designed extension. See our guides to building regulations for a house extension and house extension cost in 2026.

Party walls, boundaries and neighbours

A conservatory built against a shared wall or with foundations close to a neighbour's property may need notice under the Party Wall etc. Act 1996. Building close to a boundary also affects the permitted eaves height. Glazing facing a neighbour can raise overlooking concerns, so where planning permission is needed, councils may ask for obscure glazing on the side.

Should you get a lawful development certificate?

A lawful development certificate confirms in writing that the conservatory is permitted development. It is optional, but useful if the design is close to a limit or you want clear evidence when you sell. It needs a site plan showing boundary distances and elevations showing heights.

Drawings for a conservatory or orangery

  • Location and block plans showing the conservatory and its distance to boundaries.
  • Floor plan showing the separating doors and the room's size.
  • Elevations showing depth, height and eaves height.
  • Construction details where building regulations apply, especially for solid roofs and knock-throughs.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need planning permission for a conservatory?

Usually not. A conservatory is permitted development if it is up to 3 m deep on an attached house or 4 m on a detached house, no more than 4 m high, with eaves no higher than 3 m within 2 m of a boundary, and not at the front.

Does an orangery need planning permission?

An orangery follows the same planning rules as a conservatory, so it is often permitted development. It usually needs building regulations approval because its mainly solid roof means it does not count as a conservatory.

Do you need building regulations for a conservatory?

Not if it is at ground level, under 30 m², separated from the house by external quality doors or walls, and has independent heating controls. Safety glazing and electrical work must still comply.

Do I need building regulations to put a solid roof on a conservatory?

Yes. Replacing a glazed or polycarbonate roof with a solid roof needs building regulations approval, including checks that the frame and foundations can carry the extra weight.

Can I remove the doors between my house and conservatory?

You can, but the conservatory then becomes part of the house and needs building regulations approval, usually including energy improvements to the roof, walls or glazing.

How big can a conservatory be without planning permission?

Up to 3 m deep on a semi-detached or terraced house and 4 m on a detached house, or 6 m and 8 m through prior approval, with a maximum height of 4 m and no more than half the garden covered by buildings.
