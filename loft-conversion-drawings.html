Loft conversion drawings

Loft conversion drawings, from head-height check to building control.

A loft conversion is the cheapest square metre most houses will ever gain, which is exactly why so many of them go wrong. The roof space looked big enough. The stair had to go somewhere. Nobody checked the head height over the new flight until the joiner was on site.

We start every loft project by testing whether it works at all — measuring the ridge height, the existing joist depth and the run available for a compliant staircase — and only then design the conversion. If the numbers do not stack up, we would rather tell you in week one than draw a scheme you cannot build.

The four conversion types, and when each one applies

Rooflight (Velux) conversions keep the existing roof shape and simply add windows in the plane of the roof. Cheapest, least disruptive, and usually permitted development — but only viable where you already have generous ridge height.

Dormer conversions build a box out from the rear roof slope to create full-height floor area. The workhorse of British loft conversions, and the type most often covered by permitted development on a rear elevation.

Hip-to-gable conversions square off a sloping hip end into a vertical gable wall, which is transformative on semi-detached and end-of-terrace houses with hipped roofs. Frequently combined with a rear dormer.

Mansard conversions rebuild the roof at a steep angle to create nearly a full storey. The most space, the most cost, and almost always full planning permission — particularly common in London terraces and conservation areas where the roof form is controlled.

Head height, stairs and the things that actually stop projects

The rule of thumb is that you need roughly 2.2 to 2.4 metres from the top of the existing ceiling joists to the underside of the ridge before insulation and a new floor structure eat into it. Below about 2.2 metres, a standard conversion becomes difficult and you are usually looking at lowering ceilings below or a mansard.

Building regulations require a fixed staircase to a habitable loft — a loft ladder will not do — and that staircase needs 2 metres of headroom over the pitch line, reduced to 1.9 metres at the centre for a stair under a sloping roof. Finding that run inside an existing landing is the single most common design constraint we deal with.

Fire safety is the other one. Converting a two-storey house into a three-storey house changes what building control expects: a protected stair enclosure down to a final exit, fire doors to habitable rooms off the stair, and mains-wired interlinked alarms. We draw these requirements into the package rather than leaving them for site.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to the questions we are asked most often.

Do I need planning permission for a loft conversion?

Many rear dormer and rooflight conversions fall under permitted development, subject to volume allowances that are typically 40 cubic metres for a terraced house and 50 cubic metres for a semi-detached or detached house, with no extension beyond the front roof plane and no raising of the ridge. Mansards, front dormers, flats and properties in conservation areas or under Article 4 directions normally need full planning permission. We confirm which applies to your property before designing.

What head height do I need for a loft conversion?

You generally want at least 2.2 to 2.4 metres from the top of the existing ceiling joists to the underside of the ridge, because the new floor structure and roof insulation will consume a significant part of it. We measure this on survey and tell you straight away whether a standard conversion is viable.

Does a loft conversion need building regulations approval?

Yes, always — even when planning permission is not required. Building control will look at the new floor structure, fire escape and stair protection, insulation, ventilation, stair geometry and balustrades. Permitted development and building regulations are two completely separate approvals.

Can I use a loft ladder instead of a staircase?

Not for a habitable room. Building regulations require a permanent fixed staircase to any loft used as a bedroom, office or living space. A loft ladder is only acceptable where the space remains storage, and a room converted without a proper stair cannot legitimately be marketed as a bedroom.

How long do loft conversion drawings take?

Allow roughly one to two weeks for a planning or LDC set following the survey, and a similar period again for the building regulations package. Mansard schemes and conversions with awkward stair geometry usually need a little longer at design stage.

Will a loft conversion add value to my house?

It usually does, particularly where it adds a genuine double bedroom and en-suite, but the figure depends entirely on your local market and ceiling price. What we can control is that the room is properly compliant — an uncertified conversion or one without a compliant stair can actively reduce value at survey.
