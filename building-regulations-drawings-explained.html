Building regulations drawings explained

Planning permission tells you what you may build. Building regulations tell you how to build it so it does not leak, freeze, collapse or trap someone in a fire.

Building regulations 08 April 2026 8 min read

Homeowners routinely arrive at building control assuming that because planning permission was granted, the hard part is done. It is a reasonable assumption and a completely wrong one. Planning and building control are separate regimes, assessed by different people against different criteria, and permission from one has no bearing on the other.

Building regulations drawings are also the only set your builder can price accurately. That alone usually justifies producing them properly.

What the Approved Documents cover

Building regulations in England are set out in a series of Approved Documents, each dealing with one area of performance. A domestic extension or conversion will typically engage most of the following:

  • Part A — Structure. Foundations, beams, lintels, load paths. Your drawings mark the structural openings and beam positions; a structural engineer sizes them.
  • Part B — Fire safety. Escape routes, protected stairs, fire door ratings, alarm grade and category, fire-resisting construction and surface spread of flame.
  • Part C — Site preparation and moisture. Damp-proof courses and membranes, ground preparation, floor build-ups and resistance to ground moisture.
  • Part F — Ventilation. Background ventilation, extract rates in kitchens and bathrooms, and whole-house ventilation strategy.
  • Part H — Drainage and waste. Above and below-ground drainage, falls, inspection chambers, rainwater and connections.
  • Part K — Protection from falling. Stair geometry, headroom, balustrade and guarding heights, glazing safety.
  • Part L — Conservation of fuel and power. U-values, thermal bridging, airtightness, glazing ratios and heating efficiency.
  • Part M — Access. Level thresholds, door widths and WC provision.
  • Part P — Electrical safety. Notifiable electrical work certified by a competent person.

New dwellings additionally engage Part O on overheating and Part S on electric vehicle charging infrastructure, both of which are now picked up routinely at plan check.

What the drawing package actually contains

  • Fully dimensioned construction plans showing wall types, structural openings, door and window schedules and specification references.
  • Sections cut through the works showing floor, wall and roof build-ups layer by layer, with insulation thicknesses, DPC and DPM positions and cavity details.
  • A U-value schedule setting out the thermal performance of each element against the Part L target.
  • A ventilation schedule with extract rates and background ventilator sizes room by room.
  • Drainage layouts for above and below ground, with falls, gullies, chambers and connection points.
  • Fire information — escape routes, door ratings, alarm positions, compartment lines.
  • Specification notes covering materials, standards and workmanship, usually as annotations across the drawings.

Full plans or building notice?

There are two routes to building control approval for most domestic work, and the choice has real consequences.

Building notice

You give the council 48 hours' notice and start work. No drawings are submitted, and everything is judged on site as it goes up. It is fast and the fee is often lower.

The trade-off is that all the risk sits with you. If the inspector decides at second fix that something does not comply, it comes out and gets rebuilt at your cost. There is no approved specification to point at, and your builder has priced from an assumption rather than a drawing — which is exactly how a fixed price becomes a moving one.

Full plans

You submit drawings and specifications in advance. A plan checker reviews them, raises queries, and issues a decision. The statutory determination period is five weeks, extendable to two months by agreement.

It takes longer at the front and costs more in fees. What you get is an approved specification, comparable quotes from builders pricing identical information, problems resolved on paper rather than on site, and a clear documentary record for when you eventually sell.

For anything structural, any change of use, and anything you will need to evidence at conveyancing, full plans is almost always the right call. Building notice is defensible for straightforward, small, like-for-like work where nothing unusual is happening.

Who checks the work

You can use your local authority's building control department, or a private sector inspector — now regulated as a Registered Building Control Approver following the reforms that followed the Building Safety Act. Both can inspect the work and issue a completion certificate.

Private inspectors are often more flexible on site visit timing and can be quicker to respond. Local authority building control holds the enforcement powers and is sometimes preferred where a project is complex or where relations with neighbours are strained.

The completion certificate matters more than you think

The document at the end of the process is the point of the whole exercise. When you sell, the buyer's solicitor will ask for the completion certificate for any building work carried out. Not having one is a recognised conveyancing problem that can delay or reduce a sale.

Where work was done historically without approval, the routes back are a regularisation application — which involves opening up completed work for inspection — or, in narrower circumstances, indemnity insurance. Both are more expensive and more disruptive than getting it right at the time.

A note on timing

Building regulations drawings are best started once the planning position is settled but before you go out to tender. Producing them earlier risks redrawing if planning requires changes; producing them later means your builders are pricing incomplete information and you lose the ability to compare quotes meaningfully.
